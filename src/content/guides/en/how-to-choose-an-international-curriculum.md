"Which international curriculum should we choose?" is the most common question in international education — and one of the most frequently answered badly. The usual answers take one of two forms: a recommendation that some system is simply "best", or a list of pros and cons. Neither answers the family's actual question.

The real question is not "which system is best" but **which system fits this student, under these conditions**.

## Separate four things that get conflated

Before any comparison, four categories of information have to be pulled apart. Merging them distorts every judgement that follows.

- **Curriculum systems** — DSE, IB, AP, A-Level, IGCSE. These determine what a student studies and how they are assessed.
- **Qualification examinations** — SAT, ACT. Used in admissions evaluation; not curricula in themselves.
- **Language tests** — IELTS, TOEFL. Evidence language proficiency, assessed separately from academic study.
- **University admissions** — application systems and processes such as UCAS or JUPAS.

These four things have entirely different timelines, preparation methods and roles. Comparing across categories is a leading source of poor decisions.

## Question 1: What is the student's academic shape?

Different systems ask for different things, and the difference is usually **structural** rather than a matter of difficulty.

- Some students thrive on sustained depth in a small number of subjects.
- Some students need to move between several disciplines to stay engaged.
- Some have strengths concentrated in mathematics and sciences; others in language and writing.

A-Level and AP sit closer to the first pattern; the IB and DSE closer to the second. That is a structural observation, not a ranking.

## Question 2: What year is it now?

System choice depends heavily on **where the student currently is**.

- Around ages 14–16: the priority is usually completing IGCSE or an equivalent stage, which exists to preserve breadth.
- Around 16: the post-16 system has to be chosen, and switching later carries a real cost.
- Already in a post-16 programme: the useful conversation is no longer "change system" but "optimise subject combination and application strategy within this one".

Establishing the current point in time eliminates roughly half of the theoretically available options.

## Question 3: Is the destination already decided?

If the family is committed to the UK, A-Level is the most direct route. If the US is the clear focus, AP and the IB are more common. If several countries or regions need to stay open, the IB and DSE generally offer wider coverage.

One caveat: **an undecided destination is not a weakness, but it changes the criteria**. When the destination is open, keeping optionality matters more than optimising a single route.

## Question 4: What can the school actually deliver?

This is the most commonly overlooked factor and one of the most consequential.

- Does the school offer the full subject range for that curriculum?
- Are the teaching staff and laboratory facilities for the relevant subjects stable?
- How have previous cohorts adapted to the programme?
- Can the school deliver internal assessment or coursework requirements properly?

The same curriculum can be a very different experience at different schools. A curriculum's name is not evidence of teaching quality.

## Question 5: Does the assessment model match the student?

Assessment design is the most underestimated difference between systems.

- **Concentrated public examinations** (DSE, A-Level): a single set of results carries most of the weight, requiring sustained exam preparation.
- **Examinations combined with internal assessment** (IB): projects, essays and internal assessment also count, requiring continuous engagement.
- **Independent single-subject examinations** (AP): effort can be concentrated on strengths, but the overall result is a set of disconnected subject scores.

Matching the assessment model matters more than matching the curriculum name. A student who struggles under high-stakes concentrated examination often performs closer to their true level in a system with assessment buffers.

## Question 6: What is the family's longer-term plan?

Whether the family expects to stay in one place, may relocate, how the budget is structured, and whether a return to a local system is possible at any stage.

These considerations feed into one key measure: **how reversible is the path**. Some systems cost a great deal to switch out of mid-course; others leave more room.

## The answers only mean something together

The six questions carry no fixed weighting, but writing them down and answering each one usually produces one of two outcomes:

1. **One system clearly leads on four or more questions** — the direction is essentially settled.
2. **Two systems are close and the trade-offs are real** — at this point the productive move is to stop debating systems and go verify school provision and subject combinations instead.

## A common trap: treating "recognition" as the only criterion

Recognition does differ between systems, but for most destinations the practical difference is far smaller than people assume. What actually determines outcomes is the level a student reaches within their chosen system — not the system's name.

A more effective order is therefore: confirm the destination countries first, then school provision, then assessment fit, and only then compare the marginal differences in recognition.

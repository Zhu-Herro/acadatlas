A-Level's structure gives subject choice unusual weight: with only three or four subjects, **every choice either narrows or preserves future options**.

Choosing subjects is not simply a matter of picking favourites, nor of picking the easiest route to high grades. It is closer to solving a problem under a set of constraints.

## Start with the constraints

Before discussing interest, list the hard constraints.

### Constraint 1: does the target programme require specific subjects?

Many university programmes state required A-Level subjects. Science and engineering routes commonly require Mathematics, sometimes with Further Mathematics or Physics; medicine-related routes usually require Chemistry and often Biology. These requirements are set by each institution and programme and **must be confirmed against the official published entry requirements**.

If the direction is not yet settled, the strategy changes: preserving combination options matters more than locking in early.

### Constraint 2: does the school offer the subject?

This is frequently overlooked. Whether the school provides full teaching for a subject — with adequate staff and laboratory facilities — determines whether the choice is realistic at all.

### Constraint 3: which awarding body, and therefore which paper structure?

The same subject can differ between awarding bodies in paper structure, assessment weighting and content emphasis. Schools normally work with a fixed board, so the effective options depend on school arrangements.

## Then consider direction alignment

With constraints established, look at alignment. The patterns below are **structural illustrations only, not programme-specific advice**:

- **Mathematics and engineering**: Mathematics as the base, usually with Further Mathematics, Physics or Chemistry. This combination demands sustained mathematical ability.
- **Medicine and life sciences**: Chemistry usually central, often with Biology; some institutions also require Mathematics or Physics.
- **Economics and business**: Mathematics is typically important, often with Economics, Further Mathematics or a business-related subject.
- **Humanities and social sciences**: common combinations include History, Economics, Politics, Geography and Psychology, with emphasis on writing and argument.
- **Languages and literature**: common combinations include English Literature, a modern language and History.

**These are common patterns, not requirements.** The actual requirements are those published by the target institution and programme.

## Two ways to build a three-subject combination

**Focused.** All three subjects point in the same direction — Mathematics, Further Mathematics and Physics, for instance. The advantage is a clear direction and greater depth; the cost is limited room to change course later.

**Mixed.** Two focused subjects plus one complementary — Mathematics, Physics and Economics, for instance. This keeps the main direction while retaining some flexibility.

Neither is inherently better. It depends on how settled the student's direction is.

## On the fourth subject

Whether a fourth subject is needed depends on the requirements of the target institutions and programmes, not on a general instinct that more is better.

A fourth subject usually serves one of two purposes: strengthening academic evidence in a specific direction, or retaining room to adjust direction. It also means a larger time commitment, which can affect performance in the other subjects.

## When to be especially careful

In these situations, prioritise preserving the ability to change direction:

- The student has not settled on a field.
- The target direction spans two areas — technology and business, for example.
- The student is weak in a subject that happens to be required for a given direction.

In these cases, locking all three subjects into a single direction makes a change of direction very expensive.

## Three common misconceptions

**Misconception one: choose subjects that are easy to score in.**
Institutions and programmes recognise subjects differently. An "easy" subject unrelated to the intended field usually does not count towards a valid combination.

**Misconception two: choose purely by interest, ignoring requirements.**
Interest matters, but hard subject requirements do not bend to interest. Both have to be satisfied.

**Misconception three: copy someone else's combination.**
Academic profiles, target institutions and school provision all differ, so the workable combination differs too. The reference value is limited.

## A workable process

1. List two or three plausible fields of study.
2. Check the A-Level subject requirements for each on the target institutions' websites.
3. Collect the "required subjects" into a set and find the intersection.
4. Use the remaining places for subjects that are both relevant and strengths.
5. Confirm with the school that those subjects are actually offered.
6. Keep one subject that complements the main direction as room to adjust.

The most common question in international admissions planning is "where can my results get me?" It is hard to answer because it compresses three distinct things into one sentence: **route relationships, application systems, and specific entry requirements**.

This page deals with the first, and explains why the other two have to come from official sources.

## Route relationships: systems and destinations

Different systems lead to different countries and regions — but the mapping is not exclusive.

- **DSE**: centred on Hong Kong, also used for applications to the UK, Australia and Canada.
- **IB**: the broadest coverage; accepted across Europe, North America, Oceania and Asia.
- **AP**: centred on the United States, accepted in Canada and at selected institutions elsewhere.
- **A-Level**: centred on the UK, also accepted in Hong Kong, Australia, Canada and Singapore.
- **IGCSE**: rarely used alone for university entry; primarily an articulation stage.

These relationships are shown graphically on the pathways page. The important caveat: **a line on that diagram means a route exists, not that admission is easy**.

## Application systems: routes in another form

Destinations differ in the systems they use, which is where route differences become concrete.

- **Hong Kong**: local students apply to local institutions mainly through JUPAS.
- **United Kingdom**: undergraduate applications are submitted through UCAS.
- **United States**: many institutions use a common application platform, while others run their own.
- **Canada and Australia**: mostly institution-specific systems, with some provincial platforms.

These differences affect timing: opening dates, limits on the number of choices, and submission deadlines all vary between systems.

## Entry requirements: why we do not publish scores

This is a deliberate boundary.

Entry requirements change every year and vary by **institution, programme, applicant profile and application round**. The same programme at the same university can change its requirement for the same qualification from one year to the next. Some institutions also set separate requirements for specific subjects.

Any score frozen onto a web page therefore goes stale quickly and can mislead. Our approach instead:

- Describe structure and route relationships, which are stable over time.
- Attach a source and an applicable academic year to every time-sensitive claim.
- Direct readers back to the official publications of the institution and admissions body.

## Three categories to keep separate during planning

### 1. Academic results

Produced by assessment within the curriculum system: public examination results, internal assessment and coursework. These are the core of an application.

### 2. Standardised and language results

Standardised tests such as SAT and ACT provide a common reference; language tests such as IELTS and TOEFL evidence proficiency. Neither is a curriculum, and their roles differ.

### 3. Non-academic material

Personal statements, references, activity records and portfolios. The weight given to these varies by country — US institutions typically assess more holistically, while UK institutions focus more on academic results and evidence of academic interest.

Planning these three separately is more effective than treating "preparing an application" as one undifferentiated task.

## Structural differences in timing

Result release dates differ between systems, and application deadlines differ between platforms. This produces predictable tensions:

- UK-oriented candidates usually submit before results are released.
- US-oriented candidates often need standardised tests completed early in the final years.
- Multi-region plans require mapping out each system's timeline earlier than single-region plans.

**Exact deadlines are published annually — always confirm against the official source.**

## A more effective planning order

1. **Decide the range of countries or regions** (not specific institutions yet).
2. **Confirm how institutions in that range treat the current curriculum.**
3. **Check whether the result release timing aligns with the application deadlines.**
4. **Plan standardised and language test scheduling.**
5. **Only then research programme-specific requirements and score ranges.**

The advantage of this order: the first three steps determine feasibility and timing, and are hard to change once set. The last two are flexible as results come in. Handling the inflexible parts first leaves more tolerance for everything else.
